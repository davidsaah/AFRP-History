The Family, Reconstructed

Rāshid El-Haddadeen Al-Ghassani and his descendants — the whole story from Adam and Eve to the city of today, drawn from the eighty volumes now on the shelf.

American Federation of Ramallah, Palestine · the Family Tree Project · 16 August 2026 · fifteen figures · 106 notes

Every stretch of this history is marked with what holds it up, and every claim carries its source. The figures are drawn from the documents themselves — the Ottoman registers, the censuses, the inscriptions, the deed of 1279. Where the record is empty the page says so and shows the shape of the emptiness, because a family history that hides its gaps cannot be trusted about anything else.

Part One

The Line

Where the family says it comes from, who made the claim, and what a genealogy of this kind was for.

The family chart begins with Adam. Before anything else is said about it, the form should be recognised for what it is. Medieval peoples across three continents wrote their descent back to a universal ancestor: the House of Wessex to Woden and thence to Noah in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle under the year 8551; the Irish to Míl; the Georgian kings to a grandson of Noah. What such a chart asserts is membership in the human story, not a chain of verified fathers. Reading it as the latter and finding it wanting is a category error.

Seven links carry the chart from Adam to Ramallah, and they do not rest on the same kind of thing. The first twenty generations — Adam to Eber — are Genesis 5 and 112. The three surviving recensions of that text disagree with one another about the ages of the patriarchs, and therefore about the date of the creation, by as much as 1,366 years (Figure 2). For a Rūm Orthodox family the Septuagint reckoning is the inherited one, which is itself a piece of family history3. These are scripture, not chronology, and were never intended as chronology.

The second link is the join, and it is the one a sceptic always aims at. Genesis 10:25–29 gives Eber two sons, Peleg and Joktan, and names thirteen sons of Joktan — among them Hazarmaveth, which is Ḥaḍramawt, and Sheba, which is Sabaʾ4. Scripture itself places this branch in South Arabia. The identification of Joktan with Qaḥṭān was made by the classical Arab genealogists and stated flatly by al-Ṭabarī about 915 CE5. It is a thousand years old and the family did not make it. Every Qaḥṭānī Arab in the world stands on that link. It is not ours to defend.

Qaḥṭān to Jafna — a literary system, not a register

From Qaḥṭān the chain runs Yaʿrub → Yashjub → Sabaʾ → Kahlān → al-Azd → Ghassān → Jafna. Modern scholarship dates this system to roughly 660–900 CE6: inherited medieval learning, assembled after Islam to order the Arab tribes into a single tree. Its weakest stretch is not the biblical join at all — it is steps 16 to 24, Yaʿrub through al-Azd — and the Arab tradition itself says so. The great compendia disagree with each other openly and by name: Ibn al-Kalbī (d. 819)7, Ibn Ḥazm (d. 1064)8, Ibn Durayd (d. 933)9, and later the digest of al-Suwaydī10, whose pages 17–18 preserve Ibn Ḥazm’s observation that Tanūkh, al-ʿUtq and Ghassān are composite tribes not descended from a single father — which weakens any claim of a linear Ghassanid pedigree from inside the tradition.

The family’s own chart, in full

All of that is the scholarly frame. The document at the centre of it is John Aziz Mogannam’s chart of thirty-six named generations — the one that hangs in the front of the family’s own telling, in Arabic and English, from آدم وحواء to Rāshid. It deserves to be printed in full rather than summarised, because it is the primary object of this whole enquiry: everything else in this history is an attempt to test it fairly.11

Read it with the grading on and the shape of the argument becomes visible at a glance. Generations 1–13 are Genesis. Generation 14 is the join — Kahtaan ibn Aaber, Qaḥṭān son of Eber, the single link where the Hebrew and Arabic systems are welded together. Generations 15–32 are the classical Arab genealogists, and it is worth noticing that the chart’s الأزد at 23, مازن at 24 and عمرو مزيقيا at 29 are exactly the names Ibn Ḥazm and Ibn al-Kalbī argue about. Generations 33–34 are attested history: Jabalah bin al-Harith and al-Harith bin Jabalah are Procopius’s Arethas and his house, men with Byzantine offices and inscriptions. Generation 35 is one line standing for nine hundred years. And generation 36 is Rāshid, where the Ottoman paper begins.

Two observations follow, and both are gifts rather than corrections. First, the chart itself is honest — it prints its own void, in the same typeface as everything else, and does not pretend the gap is full. Second, the name Al-Ghassānī enters at generation 29, with ‘Amr Muzayqiyāʾ, which is precisely where the classical sources put the naming at the spring12. The family’s chart and the ninth-century genealogists are telling the same story in the same order. That is a real finding, and it is a point in the chart’s favour.

And they disagree about something more fundamental. Ghassān is not an ancestor. It is a spring.

إنما سُمّوا غسان بماء نزلوه، ليس بأب ولا أمّ
“They named the children of Jafna ‘Ghassān’ after a water at which they alighted — it is neither a father nor a mother. Whoever drank of this water was called Ghassānī, and the name of the water is Ghassān.” Ibn Durayd, al-Ishtiqāq13 — and Ibn al-Kalbī says the same, locating the water between Zabīd and Rimaʿ14. The Ghassanids’ own poet, Ḥassān ibn Thābit: الأزد نسبتنا والماء غسّان — “al-Azd is our lineage, and the water is Ghassān.”15

Three of the tradition’s greatest authorities draw the boundary in three different places — Ibn al-Kalbī at Māzin b. al-Azd, taking in the Anṣār of Medina; Ibn Ḥazm at only the four sons of ʿAmr Muzayqiyāʾ who drank, excluding Aws and Khazraj by name; Ibn Durayd at the children of Jafna alone16. This does not weaken the family’s claim so much as change its kind. Ghassanid identity was never a bloodline running out of one man. It was a people who arrived somewhere and were named for it — which is, on reflection, exactly the sort of thing a family that walked from Karak to a ridge in these hills ought to recognise.

The sharpest critic is inside the tradition

Ibn Khaldūn (1332–1406) is the most searching sceptic of deep genealogy anyone has produced, and he is an Arab historian writing in Arabic about Arab pedigrees17. His argument is that lineage is a real social fact with a short half-life: kinship binds while it is felt, and the memory of a common ancestor beyond a handful of generations becomes an assertion rather than a knowledge. He names this branch of the tree. The honest position holds his critique and the family’s claim at the same time — and that is the position this history takes.

Which brings us to a number. The chart’s own void reads “Unknown Al-Ghassani — 45 generations.” The gap it spans runs from Jafna, floruit about 520, to Rāshid, floruit about 1500 — call it a thousand years. Forty-five generations across a thousand years implies a male-line generation of 22.2 years, which no observed population sustains; the range for male lines is about 30 to 3518. At 32 years the same gap gives about 31 generations. Correcting the count does not close the void — it measures it honestly.

Part Two

The Road, and Nine Hundred Years of Silence

The Ghassanids were real, and their one hard trace sits on the plateau the family remembers. Then the record stops.

Strip away the later literature and what remains of the Ghassanids is small, hard, and more interesting than the legend. Al-Ḥārith ibn Jabala, phylarch 528–569, is recorded by Procopius — a contemporary who disliked him — as the man Justinian set over “as many clans as possible” of the Arabs of the East19. He held the Byzantine office of phylarch and the honorific patrikios: Greek titles, Roman pay. In 542 he petitioned the empress Theodora for bishops for the persecuted Miaphysites, which produced the consecration of Jacob Baradaeus — the best-attested fact tying the dynasty to non-Chalcedonian Christianity20.

The rest is stones rather than chronicles: the audience hall outside Ruṣāfa with its Greek acclamation; inscriptions at Qaṣr al-Ḥayr naming “Flavius Arethas, patrikios”; a building inscription in the Ḥawrān; and a church at Tall al-ʿUmayrī invoking al-Mundhir21. And a church at Nitl, a few kilometres south-east of Madaba — which is to say, on the plateau the family’s tradition names as the road. The best physical evidence for Ghassanid Christianity and the family’s remembered geography turn out to describe one landscape. That is not a proof of descent. It is a striking convergence, and it should be stated as exactly that.

One caution belongs here, and it comes from inside the scholarship. Much of what circulates about the Ghassanids derives from Irfan Shahîd’s volumes22, and Greg Fisher’s critique is substantial: a “kingdom” that was really a set of allied individuals; an identity read backwards from later sources; buildings inferred from a book-list that no excavation has produced; a sedentarisation that is not attested23. No contemporary Greek, Syriac or Latin source ever uses the word “Ghassanid.” They name individuals. Our ability to speak of a Ghassanid people at all comes from the Arabic tradition — the same tradition that says Ghassān is a spring.

The dam, and a date that has to move

The family tradition dates the departure to the bursting of the Maʾrib dam, about 300 AD. The dam is real, its breaches are dated, and the dates do not match: the recorded breaches are 449–450 and 543–548, with final collapse later in the sixth century. Abraha’s great inscription of 548 CE — CIH 541 — records the repair and names the tribes and embassies present, and it is free to read2425. This is a correction, not a demolition: the migration tradition survives; the century attached to it moves.

And then the void

From the last Jafnid to the first Ḥaddādīn there is no record of any kind, and the family’s own chart is the thing that says so. Nine hundred years. Four great chronicles that should have caught something were searched in full, with working controls, and all four are silent: Yaḥyā al-Anṭākī26, Agapius of Manbij27, Michael the Syrian28, and Bar Hebraeus29. So are Nasrallah’s survey of Melkite literature30, Sanjian’s Armenian colophons31 and Agnes Smith Lewis’s Sinai catalogue32.

There is a structural reason for part of that silence, and it is worth printing because it looks like absence and is not. Empty colophons are evidence about naming, not about population. A man of ʿAbūd who entered Mar Saba became “al-Maqdisī”; the monastery, not the village, supplied the name33. The Syriac silence is structural too: the West Syrian diocesan lists do not cover a Melkite countryside.

And the window is not empty — it is only anonymous. At Jifnā in 1179, nine kilometres from Ramallah, a bilingual Greek and Arabic building inscription at the monastery of Choziba names Ibrāhīm and his brothers, sons of Mūsā, of Jifna: ordinary laymen of a hill village, named in stone, paying for work on a monastery in the middle of the Crusader century34. At ʿAbūd, a Syriac inscription of 1058 names a patriarch, an archbishop and a priest, and three of the village’s monks appear in manuscript colophons; its church, excavated by Hamdan Taha, has four building phases from the fifth century to the eighteenth35. In 1374 a Christian of Jifnā, Niʿma b. Bishāra, appears in the Ḥaram al-Sharīf documents36. East of the Jordan the community is continuous: the pilgrim Thietmar, on his journey of 1217–18, was hosted and fed at al-Shawbak by a Frankish widow living in the suburb, where Christians and Muslims lived side by side; Abū al-Fidāʾ records a Christian majority at Shawbak in 1321; Karak’s street plan still carries a Burj al-Naṣārā37.

None of these men is an ancestor. Together they prove that the population the family claims to come from was here, had names, and wrote them down.

Part Three

The People of the Land

Canaanites, Philistines, Samaritans — and the question of who was here before the family arrived, which turns out to have an answer.

A family history that begins with Adam has to say something about the ground it ends on. The hills around Ramallah were not empty in 1562 and were never empty; the question is what relationship the people who walked up from Bayt Jālā have to the people who had been farming these terraces for four thousand years. Until recently that question could only be answered rhetorically. It can now be answered with evidence — and the evidence is unusually clear.

The Canaanites, and what happened to them

The city-states of this landscape are visible in the fourteenth century BCE in the Amarna letters, the cuneiform correspondence of local rulers with the Egyptian court — Jerusalem, Shechem, Gezer, Lachish, writing in Akkadian about their neighbours and their troubles38. These are the Canaanites, and the old question was whether they were displaced. The genomic answer is that they were not. Agranat-Tamir and colleagues sequenced seventy-three individuals from Bronze Age sites across the southern Levant and found a single, coherent population39; Haber and colleagues had already shown that present-day Levantine populations derive the large majority of their ancestry from those Bronze Age Canaanites, with continuity across five millennia40. The population did not leave. It changed language and religion, repeatedly, and stayed.

The Philistines — a two-century signal

The Philistines are the best-documented case of newcomers to this coast, and their fate is the most instructive thing in this section. Ancient DNA from Ashkelon shows a European-derived component arriving with the early Iron Age — a real migration, visible in the genome. And then: “within no more than two centuries, this genetic footprint introduced during the early Iron Age is no longer detectable.”41 They did not vanish; they were absorbed. The incomers married in, and within about eight generations the local population had simply taken them up.

That is worth holding beside this family’s own story. A group arrives from elsewhere, carrying a name and a memory, and within a few centuries is indistinguishable from the people already there — that is not a threat to the Ghassanid claim, it is the ordinary mechanism of this landscape, and it has happened at least four times on this ridge. The Ḥaddādīn who came up from Bayt Jālā in 1562 were the latest instance of a very old pattern, not an exception to it.

The Samaritans — the neighbours who never left

And one community did not change language or religion, and is still here: the Samaritans of Nablus, thirty miles north of Ramallah, whose community has been continuous on Mount Gerizim since antiquity and whose chronicles are their own historiography42. They matter here for two reasons. They are the living proof that continuity on this land is not a rhetorical claim but an observable fact — a community that can be traced in place across two thousand years. And their villages are the immediate northern neighbours of the district in which Ramallah sits; the ridge road that carries this whole story runs from Hebron through Jerusalem and al-Bīra to Nablus, and the Samaritan community sits at its northern end.

The place-names are the argument

The most durable evidence of continuity is not in a laboratory. It is in the names of the villages themselves. Arabic toponymy across Palestine preserves Canaanite and Aramaic forms — the sound of a language that stopped being spoken a thousand years before Arabic arrived, carried forward by the people who kept farming the same terraces. Nur Masalha builds his four thousand years of Palestinian history on exactly this argument43, and the Palestinian gazetteer literature is what makes it checkable: Shukrī ʿArrāf’s survey of the geographical names of Palestine44, al-Dabbāgh’s بلادنا فلسطين45, and Muhammad Maraqten’s study of place-name memory46.

The family’s own district supplies the example. Rām Allāh itself is built from رام, a Semitic root meaning height — the same root as Hebrew rām and Aramaic rāmā, and al-Dabbāgh says so explicitly47. The name is not an Arabic invention of 1562 laid over blank ground; it is a Canaanite-era word for a high place, still doing its job, in the mouths of the people who never left. That the deed of 1279 already carries the name in that form (Part Four) is the documentary end of the same argument.

So the honest statement of this family’s relation to the land has two halves, and both are stronger than the usual rhetoric. By tradition, the family came from Yemen through Ghassān and Karak, and that story is consistent with everything testable in it. By descent, the people of these hills — including whoever the Ḥaddādīn married into over twenty-one generations — carry the ancestry of the Bronze Age population of this same country. Both can be true at once. Arriving from somewhere and belonging to a place are not opposites here; they are, on this ridge, the same process seen at two different distances.

Part Four

The Hills Before Ramallah

The ground the family arrived on was not empty. It had been Christian, then Frankish, then Mamluk — and one of its churches sits two kilometres from the town centre.

Ramallah sits at about 850 metres on the watershed ridge, and almost everything about its history follows from that. The central highlands are crossed north to south by a single watershed route — Hebron, Bethlehem, Jerusalem, al-Bīra, Nablus — and a village on that ridge is on a road whether it wants to be or not. It also sits on a seam: the district was administratively split, Sinjil and the eastern hills under Jerusalem, Wādī Banī Zayd and the western hills under al-Ramla, with Ramallah’s site on the boundary48. That is why the record here is dark. The Mamluk postal system makes the same point from the other side: there is no barīd station between Ludd and Jinīn, the whole hill country bypassed49.

What the ground shows is continuous Christian settlement through exactly the centuries the ancestry chart cannot document — and the excavation is Palestinian. A Byzantine church at Khirbet et-Tireh, two kilometres from Ramallah’s centre, has been dug across a decade of seasons by Salah Hussein al-Houdalieh of al-Quds University: the eastern church, its mosaic pavements and their substrates, an oil-press complex, a clay bread stamp, and the agricultural watchtowers around it5051. This is the closest thing the town has to a published archaeological record of its own ground.

Yāqūt saw al-Bīra

The Arabic geographical tradition names four of these villages while the imperial chancery names none — and one entry is an eyewitness. Yāqūt describes the Palestinian al-Bīra and says plainly رأيتها, I saw it, recording its destruction by Saladin52. That correction matters methodologically, and it was established by this project: the old working rule that al-Bīra in Arabic always means the Euphrates town holds only for two later authors. A second correction of the same kind: Mujīr al-Dīn has five occurrences of البيرة, not six — three in his text and two editorial, one of those Ilbīra in Spain53.

Two miles away, al-Bīra is the best-documented village in this hill country in the whole medieval period — as Magna Mahumeria, a Frankish settlement of the Holy Sepulchre. The cartulary charter of 11 February 1156 rolls ninety-two burgesses, with some fifty more names added over the following three decades, implying a Frankish population of perhaps 500–70054. It names nobody local. Pringle gives the parish church of St Mary as entry no. 66, with a restored plan55. The Franks are documented; the villagers among whom they lived are not.

A verified negative, and then a deed

In 1495 Mujīr al-Dīn finished al-Uns al-jalīl, the standard late-medieval topography of the Jerusalem district. It was searched directly and in full. There is no Ramallah in it. That negative is a finding, and it still stands.

And yet the name is older than the town. A boundary case heard in the Jerusalem sharīʿa court in June 1565 turns on a Mamluk endowment deed of 678 AH — 1279 CE — in which Rām Allāh is already a surveyed place.

وقد أحضر حمزة جلبي كتاب الوقف المؤرخ سنة ٦٧٨ هـ وقف السلطان منصور قلاوون…
“…and Ḥamza Chelebi produced the waqf deed dated the year 678, the endowment of Sultan al-Manṣūr Qalāwūn… and it appeared that the boundaries of Rām Allāh are: on the south Dayr Fazṭā, on the east the travelled road, its limit the Silsila al-Rummāniyya, running north to Burj Salmiyya, which is the northern boundary of Rām Allāh.” Jerusalem sharīʿa register 48, p. 54 · 23 Dhū al-Qaʿda 972 / 22 June 156556. Al-Dabbāgh, working from Arabic sources with no access to the register, records the same endowment independently57.

That is 283 years before the traditional founding. It does not make the founding story wrong. It makes it one layer of a longer one.

Part Five

The Founding

For the first time the story rests on paper. The Ottoman state counted taxpayers village by village, and the registers date the founding more precisely than the family’s own account does.

Sameeh Hammoudeh worked through the Ottoman tax registers for Ramallah and published the result in 201458. It is the most important documentary research ever done on the founding, and it is by a Palestinian scholar working on Palestinian ground. His monograph رام الله العثمانية: دراسات في تاريخها الاجتماعي ١٥١٧–١٩١٨ extends it59.

The sequence runs like this. In 1525–28 Ramallah is uninhabited — listed as agricultural land of the Ibrāhīmī waqf of Hebron, assessed at 3,000 aqja, with no households at all. Note how that dovetails with the deed of 1279: the site is endowment land, and has been for centuries. In 1538–39 four Muslim households are counted, the first people on the site. In 1553–54 there are six Muslim households here — and thirty-six Christian households at Bayt Jālā, eleven miles south, where the group called the Kasābra is registered. In 1562 the Christians arrive: twenty-seven more families come up from Bayt Jālā with eight unmarried men, about thirty-seven Christian households against ten Muslim. That is the founding, and a document carries it. By 1596–97 the village has eighty households, seventy-one of them Christian, taxed at a quarter and yielding 9,400 aqja60. The registers name names: the Ḥaddādīn, al-Kasābra and al-Naqqāsh among the Christians; al-Labūd and Abū al-Thanāyā among the Muslims.

Two corrections, and neither one weakens the family

The founding is 1562, not “about 1550.” This is a promotion, not a demotion: a document carries 1562 and nothing carries 1550. And the migrants were probably Rāshid’s sons or grandsons rather than Rāshid himself — the register puts the movers a generation or two below him. The family’s account is refined by the paper, not refuted by it.

The road, written down in Jordan

The family remembers a route: the Ḥijāz, Udhruḥ, al-Shawbak, al-Karak, Maʿīn, and across the Jordan. Every stage from Karak onward is attested independently, in registers kept in another country. Karak’s Christian community is documented from the early thirteenth century and counted at 103 Christian households and 8 bachelors in the register of 159661. And Peake’s Karak clan register records the Ḥaddādīn directly:

…وتزوج فتاة من عشيرة الحدادين، ومذهبهم روم أرثوذكس
“…he came to al-Karak and married a girl of the Ḥaddādīn clan, and their rite is Greek Orthodox. They dwell in al-Karak and the village of Ḥamūd, and they have relatives in Nazareth called al-Hanādisa.” F. G. Peake, تاريخ شرقي الأردن وقبائلها62. The syntax matters: an incoming clan married into the Ḥaddādīn, which puts the Ḥaddādīn there first.

Peake also records a Ḥaddādīn ḥamūla scattered across al-Ḥuṣn, Aydūn, Kufr ʿAwān and al-Salṭ — an old, fragmented lineage rather than one family. That is what the evidence supports, and it is a more interesting fact than a single heroic migration.

The tribe that stayed

This is worth dwelling on, because it is usually left out of the Ramallah telling. The Ḥaddādīn did not all leave. The clan is a living Jordanian Christian tribe: concentrated at al-Karak and the village of Ḥamūd, with branches at al-Ḥuṣn, Aydūn and Kufr ʿAwān in the north, at al-Salṭ in the centre, and relatives at Nazareth called al-Hanādisa — and the Karak Christian community from which they come is documented continuously from Thietmar’s Frankish widow at Shawbak in 1217–18 to the 103 Christian households and 8 bachelors counted at Karak in the register of 1596.63 The Ramallah branch is an emigrant fragment of a tribe that is still there.

The clan has its own historians on that side of the river, and they should be read alongside the Ramallah ones. Munther J. Haddadin — the Jordanian water minister and negotiator — has written both the tribal history, دولة الغساسنة: أصيلها ورحيلها (2024), and a full clan volume, عشيرة الحدادين: أصولها وفروعها (2025)64. ʿAbbās Ḥaddādīn’s clan history, written in Jordan, records the road independently of anything in Ramallah: “they were in southern Jordan, specifically al-Shawbak, and they had holdings in Udhruḥ… the two clans went out from al-Shawbak to al-Karak.”65 Two branches of one clan, separated for four hundred years and writing in two countries, describe the same route in the same order. That convergence is not proof, but it is the kind of evidence oral tradition can actually supply, and it is stronger than either telling on its own.

The Palestinian side of the same literature is al-Dabbāgh’s volume on the Arab tribes and their lineages66, with Kaḥḥāla’s tribal encyclopaedia67 and al-ʿAzīzī’s Jordanian compendium68 beside it.

Rāshid, and what a clan actually was

The tradition holds that Rāshid al-Ḥaddādīn — ḥaddād, the blacksmith — had five sons, and that the town’s clans descend from them: Ḥaddād, whose own five sons produced Awad, Sharaka, Yousef, Jaghab and Azzouz; and Ḥassān, Shuqair, Ibrāhīm and Jiryis. Rafīdī joins the founder by a broken line on the family’s own structural diagram — affiliated, not a son — and the book keeps the broken line69. Ḥaddād is an occupational surname and non-diagnostic on its own; no medieval attestation of a Ḥaddādīn lineage was found in any corpus searched for this project — and that negative was searched for, not assumed.

And a ḥamūla here was a residence quarter as much as a descent group: each clan held a share of the village land, a quarter of the built-up core, and a madāfa, the guest house where its men received visitors and settled disputes. Riwaq’s building survey can still map most of those quarters on the ground.70 The Family Tree Project’s nine clan sections are exactly this structure, and the printed record now runs to more than nine thousand named descendants beneath them71. The book the whole project is built on remains ʿAzīz Shāhīn’s72.

Part Six

The Ottoman Centuries

Three hundred years in which Ramallah is a Christian village among Muslim ones — taxed by the state, ruled by shaykhs, raided in bad years, and slowly growing.

For most of these three centuries Ramallah is a name in a fiscal document and very little else. The best scholarship on the seventeenth century maps the Christian villages of the Jerusalem district one by one from the jizya registers — Felicita Tramontana’s work — and Ramallah is on the map73. Dror Zeʾevi’s study of the district in the 1600s is the other half of the picture74. Between them they are the two scholars who could fill the emptiest stretch of the whole story, and both are free.

The state taxed the hill country but rarely governed it directly. Between the state and the village stood the shaykh, and Ramallah’s neighbours held the thrones — the qurā al-karāsī, throne villages, whose families farmed the taxes of a group of villages and built in stone to show it. Riwaq counts twenty-four shaykhdoms in the central highlands75. Ramallah was not a throne village. It was one of the villages the thrones taxed.

Two corrections belong here, both established against the registers. The Barghūthī shaykhdom is not in the sixteenth-century registers — the shaykhs of Banī Zayd in 1563–76 are the house of Abū Rayyān, and the Barāghtha appear in 171076. And Banī Ḥārith is not attested in these hills before the sixteenth century. These are exactly the details a family history gets wrong by inheriting them from later tradition. The Arabic side of the shaykhly story is Iḥsān al-Nimr’s history of Jabal Nablus77.

1799, and a family story tested the same way

Napoleon marched from Cairo to Acre and back in four months in 1799. The hill country was never touched — and the walls that stopped him at Acre were rebuilt afterwards. That matters, because one Ramallah family tells this century differently: that its men were masons, taken north to build for the pasha and fetched home to build the church. The tradition was tested link by link. Volney describes Acre’s wall in 1785 as little better than a garden wall78; Buckingham describes the walls as al-Jazzār rebuilt them, at pp. 75–76 — not 73–76, as has been printed79; Seetzen finds the moat in use in August 180680; and Gosker’s excavation of the Ottoman ravelin dates the fortifications archaeologically81. The chronology allows the story. No document names the men. That is the honest verdict, and it is printed as such.

A related correction. The claim that the Orthodox church was “built to a Catholic layout” is a folk gloss. A vaulted basilican hall was the ordinary Orthodox form, and before 1856 no non-Muslim community could lawfully raise a dome — the legal regime is the Gülhane edict of 1839 and the Hatt-ı Hümayun of 1856, whose authoritative English texts are in Hertslet8283. Ramallah had no Latin church to copy until 1913. The carved 1850 portal date is solid; the “icons dated 1830” and the “Byzantine basilica” trace only to unsourced Wikipedia and should not be printed84.

1834, and the travellers

The revolt against Ibrāhīm Pasha in 1834 is the one moment in three Ottoman centuries when the hill country north of Jerusalem enters the historical record in force — conscription, revolt, and the defeat of the shaykhs who ruled Ramallah’s district. Four years later Edward Robinson arrives, and from 1838 Ramallah stops being a name in a tax register and becomes a place someone describes: he finds the inhabitants Christian, and counts 200 taxable men85. Guérin describes it around 1870 and counts 249 houses86. Conder and Kitchener put it on a proper map in 1873, at p. 13 of the third memoir87. Socin gives a figure in 1879 and Schick another in 189688.

Between 1852 and 1913 a village of a few hundred acquires the institutional furniture of a town. The Church of the Transfiguration in 1852; the Latin Patriarchate school in 1858; Eli and Sybil Jones and Mariam Karam in 1869; the Girls Training Home in 1889 with fifteen students; the Melkite church in 1895; and the Friends Boys School from 1901, its cornerstone laid in 19138990. The Quaker schools are the hinge of modern Ramallah: they produced an English-speaking, American-connected professional class two generations before the Mandate, and they are the reason the emigration to the United States ran through this town rather than another.

Part Seven

Leaving, and the Mandate

From the 1870s the town begins to export its own people, and the Ramallah of the twentieth century is built as much in Detroit and Jacksonville as on the ridge.

This is the part of the story most families in the American Federation know from the inside, and it is also where the documents are richest. Emigration from the Bethlehem area began in the 1870s and reached Ramallah shortly after. Saleh Abdel Jawad’s study of the district finds that Ramallah’s emigration had a distinctive shape: a second, family-based wave after the First World War that continued to 1948, where neighbouring al-Bīra’s was a single-male pattern91. They went to New York, Baltimore, Chicago, Detroit and San Francisco, and later to Jacksonville, Birmingham, Cleveland, Houston and Atlanta. The American Federation of Ramallah, Palestine was founded by recent immigrants in 1952 and formally federated in Detroit on 7 September 1959.

A number this history will not give: no figure could be verified for how many Ramallah people had emigrated by 1914 or by 1948. The widely repeated claim that more Ramallawis live abroad than in the town is plausible and uncited everywhere it appears. Lisa Taraki’s figures are the closest to hard numbers — about 1,500 of 6,000 had left by 1946, and over 4,000 Ramallawis were living in the United States by 196092. And one story not to borrow: the mother-of-pearl and olive-wood pilgrim trade is documented for Bethlehem and Bayt Jālā and is often transferred to Ramallah in popular accounts, with no evidence93.

Between 1908 and 1948 the town acquires the apparatus of modernity and, for the first time, is counted properly. The municipality is founded in 1908. Boulos Shehadeh, born in Ramallah in 1882, founds Mirʾāt al-Sharq in Jerusalem on 17 September 1919; its complete run of 1,770 issues carries more than 1,500 mentions of the town, with Arabic OCR, and it is the most under-used source in this entire bibliography94.

Then the censuses. Barron counts 3,104 in 192295; Mills counts 4,286 in 193196; the Village Statistics of 1945 give 5,08097. And inside Mills’s table is a fact worth pausing on: 1,941 men to 2,345 women — 404 more women than men.

In 1948 the town was on the Arab Legion side of the line. It kept its houses and lost its hinterland, and in a matter of days it stopped being a small Christian town: the refugee camps — al-Amʿari, Jalazone, Qalandiya — are established from 1949 and change its composition permanently98. The decisive event happened twenty kilometres away, and Ramallah absorbed it. The first count after the line moved is the Jordanian census of 1961, at 14,75999.

Part Eight

The City

Jordanian town, occupied town, and then — almost by accident — the seat of a government.

Two decades under Jordan after the formal annexation of April 1950; then fifty-eight years of occupation from 1967; and a university at Birzeit that made the town the intellectual centre of the West Bank. The building at the centre of Ramallah tells the political story on its own: the Muqāṭaʿa was a Tegart fort built by the British in the 1930s, then a Jordanian headquarters, then an Israeli military government building, then the seat of the Palestinian Authority. One compound, five governments — which is why the town became a capital without ever being declared one.

The numbers for the modern town are good and recent. The PCBS census of 2017 gives Ramallah at 38,998 and al-Bīra at 45,975; the 2024 projections are 43,880 and 49,657; the governorate held 279,730 at the 2007 census and is projected at 355,202100. Christians were 32 per cent of Ramallah in 1997; across Palestine they fell from 1.5 per cent of the population in 1997 to 1.0 at the 2017 census — about 46,850 people101.

What the numbers mean is contested — and the best critic is a Ramallah sociologist. Lisa Taraki’s “Enclave Micropolis” takes the “Ramallah bubble” critique seriously — the villas of al-Masyoun and al-Tireh against the Amʿari, Jalazon and Qalandiya camps, the NGO salaries, the rents — and then argues that it misreads what is happening. What looks like a bubble is also genuine urbanisation, in the only place the occupation left room for it. Her own number for the constraint: West Bank checkpoints rose from 376 in August 2005 to 540 in December 2006102.

And the history has something to say about that. Ramallah has been a substitute for somewhere else before. It was founded by people who could not stay at Karak; refounded in 1948 by people who could not stay at Lydda; and made a capital in 1995 by a government that could not sit in Jerusalem103. A town built three times by people arriving from a place they had to leave is not an accident of the last thirty years. It is the pattern of the whole story, and this family is one of its first instances.

Part Nine

The Evidence

What this reconstruction rests on, what it could not establish, and the five things that would change the picture.

Reading down the story, the grades fall as Figure 1 shows. Adam to Joktan is scripture. Joktan to Jafna is classical scholarship, datable to c. 660–900 CE: inherited, not invented here, and not the family’s to defend. The Jafnid phylarchs are attested history. Jafna to Rāshid is nothing at all, and the chart itself says so. The hills before 1562 are attested — excavated churches, a Frankish charter, a waqf deed of 1279. The founding and everything after is documents.

Where the family’s own telling stands

Eight claims were tested against the record, and most survive. Descent from the Ghassanid kings is open — no evidence either way across nine hundred years, and the claim belongs to a recognised genre. The departure “about 300 AD” is corrected. The road through the Ḥijāz, Udhruḥ, Shawbak and Karak is consistent, and its last legs are confirmed station by station in the registers. The founder and the date are refined: 1562, with the movers a generation or two below Rāshid. The Patriarchate’s grant of Khirbat Ramallah is untested — no independent documentation has been produced, and the Jerusalem court registers survive from 1529 and have never been searched for it104. The departure after a refused marriage proposal has no documentary trace, as one would expect of a family reason.

Four archives and one laboratory

None of this is settled for ever, and four of the five things that would change it could be finished in a season by one person with Arabic and a library card.

  1. Verify sijill 48, p. 54 against the page images. The volume is now in the drive. This is the highest-value half-hour available to the project, and everything in Part Five above the reconciliation depends on it.
  2. Open al-Bakhīt and al-Sawāriyya’s published Jerusalem registers, volume four. They print taxpayers by name. Do the Christian household heads at Ramallah in 1562 carry the clan names?105 Settles the founding.
  3. Then the Karak side. The ʿAjlūn defter of the same year, published in Amman in 1989. Are there Ḥaddādīn households at Karak or Maʿīn? Settles the crossing.
  4. Then the other twenty-two IRCICA volumes, and Yāqūt’s Jerusalem-district village roster mapped against the 1562 defter — a day’s work nobody has done. Fills the emptiest century, with Tramontana and Zeʾevi beside it.
  5. And in parallel, the test. Sixteen men, two per clan line, plus volunteers from the Karak Ḥaddādīn; roughly $450 each. It runs while the archives are being read. The one result nobody else can get — though note its limit honestly: a Y-DNA date can tell 600 from 1500, and it cannot tell 1200 from 1500106.

And one that will not work: no archive will name the missing generations. That is a fact about the record, and it is shared by every village family in Palestine.

What the story is

A town founded in 1562 by Christians walking up from Bayt Jālā, on a ridge that had held a church a thousand years before they got there, by a family that remembers coming from Karak and believes it came from Ghassān — on a piece of ground that already bore the name Rām Allāh in a Mamluk deed of 1279. Most of that can be documented. One stretch of it never will be. Printing the gap, and saying which is which, is what makes the rest of it worth trusting.
