## Chapter 1

### When were the terraces first built?

**Status:** RANGE · printed

No terrace wall around our town has been dated directly, because a wall that is repaired every year keeps no layers. The oldest terraces we can tie to a dated village are at Khirbet Raddana, on our own ridge, where farmers levelled the slopes around their houses about 1200 to 1000 BC. An earlier start, about 3000 BC, has been proposed but not shown. Most of the walls standing today were rebuilt, stone by stone, in the last five or six centuries.

**Sources:** Nayrūz (2004), p. 143; Ghadban (2015), pp. 39 and 42; OSL dates of standing terraces from Israeli studies (web), context only.

### How old is the Shuqba layer?

**Status:** RANGE · printed

The layer that held the Neanderthal tooth has never been dated directly. The team that studied the tooth places it, from the animal bones found with it, in the time when Neanderthals lived in this land, roughly seventy to fifty thousand years ago. That is an estimate, not a date, and a published reply disputes what the tooth was found with.

**Sources:** Blinkhorn et al., *Scientific Reports* 11 (2021), discussion. Web only; nothing on our shelf.

### How settled were the Natufians?

**Status:** RANGE · printed

Scholars now describe them as settled in some places and seasons and on the move in others. At their main sites in the hills, families stayed in stone houses for much or all of the year; in drier country and harder times they moved again. They were not the first people to stay put, but they were the first here to make a habit of it.

**Sources:** Ghadban (2015), p. 39; Edwards (1989) and Maher et al. (2012), web; Israeli site studies context only.

### What was the great tower at Jericho for?

**Status:** STILL OPEN · not printed in the box

Four readings stand: defence (Kenyon); flood works for the wall and a communal or ritual tower (Bar-Yosef 1986); a monument of those who held the spring; a solstice marker (Liran and Barkai 2011). No new evidence settles it.

**Sources and searches:** Shelf: nothing on the PPNA tower. Web: Kenyon 1957; Bar-Yosef, *Current Anthropology* 27 (1986); Liran and Barkai, *Antiquity* 85 (2011) (Israeli, context).

## Chapter 2

### Where did the Philistines’ southern-European ancestry come from?

**Status:** RANGE · printed

The genes cannot yet name the homeland. The newcomers’ ancestry matches Bronze Age people of the Greek islands, Sardinia and Spain about equally well, because those peoples were themselves alike; the Aegean is the most often favoured. What the genes do show is the ending: within two hundred years the newcomers had married into the people already here.

**Sources:** Feldman et al., *Science Advances* 5 (2019). Web only; no ancient-DNA paper on our shelf.

### Is there an Iron Age village under the old town?

**Status:** RESOLVED · printed

None has ever been reported. What has been found in the old town, a watchtower, a church and an oil press, is Crusader, and no one has dug beneath it. The Iron Age village of our ridge is Khirbet Raddana: an open village without walls, where farmers stored wheat, cast bronze in their own workshop and gathered the rain from their terraces into cisterns cut in the rock. It now lies under apartment blocks.

**Sources:** Nayrūz (2004), pp. 53 and 137–144; the Greenberg and Keinan gazetteer (Israeli), context only.

## Chapter 3

### When was Gerizim’s first sanctuary, and when did the Samaritans part from Judaism?

**Status:** RANGE · printed

The first sanctuary on Gerizim goes back to the Persian period, sometime between about 450 and 330 BC. The parting of Samaritans and Jews was not one quarrel on one day. It grew over three or four centuries, hardened when the Hasmoneans burned the sanctuary about 110 BC, and was complete by the time of the Gospels.

**Sources:** Web summaries of the excavation (Magen, Israeli, context only) and of Pummer (2016) and Knoppers (2013), which were not read for this answer.

## Chapter 4

### What became of al-Ḥārith’s family after 594?

**Status:** RANGE · printed

The family does not vanish with the Greek writers. Al-Ḥārith’s grandson Jafna was back in Roman service by 587. Al-Mundhir was still alive in exile in Sicily in July 600, when the Pope himself pleaded for him, and he came home in 602. Their people fought the Persians on their own ground in 613 and lost, and after that the house is a name in Arabic poetry until the conquest.

**Sources:** Shahîd, *BASIC* I.1 (1995), pp. 554–564, 602–605, 618–619, 636–645; Nöldeke (1887).

### When did the dam at Maʾrib finally fail?

**Status:** RANGE · printed

The last repair carved in stone is from 557/558, and the Qurʾān, revealed at Mecca before 622, already speaks of the flood as past (Sūrat Sabaʾ 34:15–16). So the dam gave way for the last time somewhere in those sixty years, most likely late in the sixth century. The familiar 570 or 575 is a later reckoning that sits inside that window.

**Sources:** Robin (2002), p. 54; Shahîd, *BASIC* II.1 (2002), pp. 18–19.

## Chapter 5

### Is there a chain of fathers between al-Ḥārith (d. 569) and the Ghassān at Gharandal (c. 890)?

**Status:** STILL OPEN · not printed in the box

No. Ibn al-Kalbī and Ibn Ḥazm carry the line only to Jabala ibn al-Ayham (two or three generations); al-Yaʿqūbī’s Ghassān at Gharandal are a tribe with no individual named. About 250 years have no named father.

**Sources and searches:** Ibn Ḥazm, *Jamhara*, Banū Jafna (scan p. 396); Nöldeke 1887, scan p. 57; Shahîd, *BASIC* II.1, p. 346 n. 174. Searched: غرندل across the shelf (Peake 1935, Dabbāgh 1979, Shahîd), Ibn al-Kalbī, Wüstenfeld, Ḥaddādīn 2024.

### Does Jabala ibn al-Ayham at the Yarmūk have a witness of his own time?

**Status:** STILL OPEN · not printed in the box

No. The battle story first appears through Ibn Isḥāq, al-Wāqidī and al-Balādhurī (8th–9th c.). Nearer his time: a lead seal in Vienna, “Gabala, patrikios”, tentatively his (Shahîd), and Ḥassān ibn Thābit’s verses mourning the Ghassanid court after the battle. Neither describes the battle.

**Sources and searches:** Shahîd, *BASIC* II.1 (2002), p. 241 and the seals list; II.2 (2010), p. 342 n. 14; web (Wikipedia), context only.

## Chapter 6

### Is Qalāwūn’s deed known?

**Status:** RESOLVED · printed

The deed itself is lost, but its terms outlived it. Later writers say it gave a tenth of the hill’s harvest to the sanctuary of Abraham at Hebron; Ottoman clerks went on collecting that share, and in 1838 the Jerusalem mosque still drew rents from Ramallah and Ṭaybeh. Two Jerusalem court records cite the deed and disagree on its year: the record of 1565 gives 678 AH (1279–80), and a record of 1644 gives 640 AH (1242–43).

**Sources:** Ḥammūdeh (2014), p. 40 and nn. 17–18, citing Rabāyiʿa (2008), p. 102; Nīrūz (2004), pp. 44–45; Robinson (1841), II, p. 513.

### When did our people make the journey from the plateau?

**Status:** RANGE · printed

No paper describes the road, but the papers mark where it ran. Christians from Karak were on the land at Kusbār, by Ḥalḥūl, after 1539. Their families were registered at Bayt Jālā by 1553–54 and stood in a Jerusalem court as people of Ramallah on 1 April 1561. So our people left the plateau between about 1539 and 1553, most likely in the 1540s. In the same years Karak lost about forty Christian households, close to the thirty-six counted at Bayt Jālā; that the two are the same families is our inference.

**Sources:** Ḥammūdeh (2014), pp. 44–48 and nn. 57–62; Abū al-Shaʿr, *Tārīkh Sharq al-Urdun*, p. 80, via Ḥammūdeh.

### Which village is on Niʿma ibn Bishāra’s contract?

**Status:** RANGE · printed

The editors read the place as “Jifnā al-Jawz” and put a question mark after it. The first word is most likely our neighbour Jifnā, a Christian village then as now: a man of Jifnā signed a Frankish charter in 1182, and another, Mūsā al-Jufnāwī, carved his name over the door of a desert monastery. What the second word means, nobody yet knows.

**Sources:** Aljoumani, Bhalloo and Hirschler (2024), pp. 48 and 175; *Survey of Western Palestine* III (1883), pp. 197–98.

### Were there Christian Ghassanids at Karak or Shawbak?

**Status:** RESOLVED · printed

Irfan Shahîd places the Ghassanid king in Moab around the year 600, in a land running from Rabba, beside Karak, to the southern end of the Dead Sea, and al-Yaʿqūbī in the ninth century still found Ghassān at Gharandal, south of Shawbak. But no writer we have read names a single Christian Ghassanid living at Karak or Shawbak in the centuries after.

**Sources:** Shahîd, *BASIC* II.1 (2002), pp. 61–62, 247–48, 346–47; Zahran (2006), pp. 149–50.

### What is in the unpublished part of the Ḥaram al-Sharīf documents?

**Status:** STILL OPEN · not printed in the box

Leads are specific but unread: Müller (2008) lists eight headmen’s crop guarantees to the Ḥaram endowment, nos. 19, 48, 110, 194, 202, 280, 348, 459, all 706–708 AH / 1306–08, villages not named; Rapoport (2025) describes 27 village documents of the 1300s and indexes Ramallah at pp. 93, 97, 99, 103–106, 109. The “twelve men and 950 dirhams” may be Ramallah (p. 105) or ʿAyn ʿArīk (p. 104).

**Sources and searches:** Müller, *MSR* 12.1 (2008), p. 188 n. 96 (PDF could not be fetched); Rapoport, *Becoming Arab* (2025), publisher’s preview only; Aljoumani et al. 2024; the McGill microfilm (OCR useless).

### Does the Syriac Chronicle of 1234 mention Ghassān?

**Status:** STILL OPEN · not printed in the box

Not in its parts translated into English (Hoyland 2011), which name the Jafnid prince personally (“Abu Jafna Nuʿman ibn al-Mundhir”) and call his people “the Christian Arabs subject to the Romans”. The Syriac text (Chabot, CSCO 81–82) is unsearched.

**Sources and searches:** Hoyland 2011, pp. 47–48, 93. Shelf: Michael the Syrian t. 1, Bar Hebraeus, Agapius — no Ghassān hit.

### The two Arabic histories of the Ḥaddādīn clans.

**Status:** STILL OPEN · not printed in the box

Neither is held. Book 1, *ʿAshāʾir al-Ḥaddādīn ḥattā ʿām 1991* (Kh., M. and ʿO. Ḥaddādīn, Amman, 1991), is known through Chatelard (2000) and Zahran (2006), who depend on it: Ṣabra chief, Rāshid his brother, the unsalted feast, Rāshid staying in Ramallah with five sons. Book 2 (attributed to ʿAbbās Ḥaddādīn) is known only by title.

**Sources and searches:** Chatelard 2000, PDF pp. 53 n. 56, 236, 268; Zahran 2006, pp. 150–52 and nn. 40–46. Web: no catalogue record for book 2.

## Chapter 7

### Who are the eighty heads of household of 1596?

**Status:** RESOLVED · printed

Naseeb Shaheen, who read the register, found that its seventy-one Christian names are the same names as in 1562, copied over unchanged after thirty-four years. To them it adds nine Muslim households. So the 1596 count is the 1562 list carried forward, not a fresh count; only the nine Muslim names are still to be read, in the published register.

**Sources:** Shaheen (2005), II, pp. 22 and 32; Ḥammūdeh (2014), p. 47.

### Where does “143 Christian households at Karak” come from?

**Status:** RESOLVED · printed

From Hind Abū al-Shaʿr’s reading of a detailed register of the ʿAjlūn district, with 11 more at Shawbak. The “970” cited with it is the register’s number, TD 970, not the year 970 AH; the register belongs to the middle of the century, about 1538. By 1596 the same town had 103 Christian households.

**Sources:** Ḥammūdeh (2014), n. 57, citing Abū al-Shaʿr, p. 80; Bani Issa (2023) for the date of TD 970.

### Was Rāshid’s father ʿĪsā or Ṣaqr?

**Status:** RANGE · printed

Our Arabic sources on both sides of the Jordan, from Khalīl Abū Rayya in 1980 to the Ḥaddādīn of Karak today, name him Ṣaqr; only one Ramallah chart names him ʿĪsā. So we follow Ṣaqr and keep ʿĪsā beside him. ʿĪsā may have been Ṣaqr’s own given name, or Ṣaqr’s father, and neither is proven.

**Sources:** Ḥammūdeh (2014), p. 43; Nīrūz (2004), pp. 186–87; al-Farḥān; the Karak diwan (2022).

### What does “1561” at the hub of the Karak wheel mean?

**Status:** RANGE · printed

Most likely the year our people first appear on paper as people of Ramallah, in the Jerusalem court record of 1 April 1561. Family writers have long quoted that year as the documented arrival, and the chart sets it beside the founder’s father as a marker for his generation. It is not his birth year.

**Sources:** Cadora, restored edition, p. 110 (foreword); Ḥammūdeh (2014), pp. 47–48.

### Is the name in the registers Ḥadīd or Ḥaddād?

**Status:** RANGE · printed

Everyone who has read the registers reads Ḥadīd, “iron”, and so does a Jerusalem court in 1604, which records a Ramallah clan called “the sons of Ḥadīd”. Sāmiḥ Ḥammūdeh takes that clan to be the Ḥadāda, the line of Rāshid’s eldest son Ḥaddād. The reading is firm; that identification is reasonable but unproven.

**Sources:** Ḥammūdeh (2014), pp. 45–49; Shaheen (2005), II, p. 27.

## Chapter 8

### Can we name the first families before 1700?

**Status:** RANGE · printed

No parish book names them, but the Ottoman clerks and the Jerusalem court did. In 1562 they wrote down Jarīr son of Ḥadīd, Sālim and Dīb sons of Jiryis, and ʿUmrān and ʿUmayra sons of Ḥassān, and in 1604 the court heard a quarrel between ʿUbayd son of Ghānim “of the sons of Ḥadīd” and Musliḥ son of Ibrāhīm “of the sons of al-Naqqāsh”. Ḥammūdeh reads these men as Rāshid’s grandsons, which would put the founders themselves about 1520–60. The first Ramallah-born priest, Yaʿqūb Ilyās, was ordained in 1700.

**Sources:** Ḥammūdeh (2014), pp. 45–49 and n. 74; Shāhīn (1982), English, chronology; Nīrūz (2004), p. 353.

### What do we know of the town from 1565 to 1800?

**Status:** RANGE · printed

The record is thin but not empty. The court heard Ramallah’s quarrels and debts in 1604 and 1605, Ramallah men helped roof the Church of the Nativity in 1671, the town had its own priest by 1700 and a paid parish school by 1706, and in the 1770s al-Jazzār took our masons to rebuild Acre. Most of the eighteenth century reaches us as family memory, and its dates differ from book to book by up to a generation.

**Sources:** Ḥammūdeh (2014), p. 49 and n. 74; *Analekta* II (1894); Nīrūz (2004), pp. 254–55, 353.

### Whose feud was it in 1820?

**Status:** RESOLVED · printed

The Shakara’s. Cadora in 1954 and Shāhīn in 1982 both tell it as a fight inside the Shakara clan, and the Abū Hānī family at its heart is a Shakara family. Shaheen’s one-line “Sharaka” is best read as a slip: the Sharaka had their own quarrel about 1850, when the whole clan left for Bayt Ijzā for a year.

**Sources:** Cadora (1954), p. 30 (restored ed., p. 41); Shāhīn (1982), English, p. 33 and chronology; Shaheen (1992), p. 23.

## Chapter 9

### Where does the story of the Rafeedie’s arrival come from?

**Status:** RESOLVED · printed

It is in print long before Ḥammūdeh. Elihu Grant, teaching in Ramallah about 1904, wrote that al-Bīra’s one Christian family, about eighty strong and kin to a Ramallah household, took its name from “a town near Nablus” and had come from there. Ibrāhīm Nīrūz heard the fuller story from the lawyer ʿĪsā Qandaḥ: a killing at Rafīdiyā about 1750, a refusal at Jifnā, a welcome in Ramallah and an invitation from al-Bīra. The year 1750 rests on that one account.

**Sources:** Grant (1907), p. 209; Nīrūz (2004), pp. 196–97.

### Where were Judge Edward Rafeedie’s parents from?

**Status:** RESOLVED · printed

His father, Fūteh (Fred) Salāmeh Rafeedie, was of al-Bīra, and his mother, Nabīha Hishmeh, was born in Ramallah on 5 May 1905. So the judge was of the al-Bīra family, and his mother of a Ramallah one.

**Sources:** Shāhīn (1982), Arabic, the Hishmeh family; Ramallah Family Tree Book v117, trees 24 and 162; Prabook (web).

### When was St Andrew’s built?

**Status:** RANGE · printed

It was begun in 1887 and opened in 1888, as the inscription over the door of its old hall records, and as Ibrāhīm Nīrūz gives it from the diocese’s own history; Shāhīn has 1887. Cadora’s 1855 is earlier than any mission worker living in Ramallah, and it most likely marks the start of the mission’s work here, not the building.

**Sources:** Nīrūz (2004), pp. 286–87, citing Faraḥ (1991), I, p. 339; Shāhīn (1982), English, p. 62.

### An account of the 1834 revolt that names Ramallah or al-Bīra, beyond Shāhīn.

**Status:** STILL OPEN · not printed in the box

No document of 1834 found. Four later accounts do name the towns: ʿĀrif al-ʿĀrif, *al-Mufaṣṣal* (1961), scan pp. 310–11 (rebels fled to Ramallah, ~150 Orthodox families; gathered at Mukhmās); Nīrūz 2004, pp. 57–58 (Ibrāhīm at Ramallah 12 April 1834; bands mustered at al-Bīra, from Ibrāhīm’s letters); Cadora 1954, pp. 41–42; Grant 1907, p. 208.

**Sources and searches:** Searched without result: Robinson 1841 II–III; Neophytos in *Analekta* III; Nimr 1938; al-Dabbāgh viii/2; Abu-Manneh; Tamari 2009; Farḥān; Kassab; the Municipality centennial book. Wanted: Rood, *JQ* 27 (2006); Rustum’s edition of the Egyptian archive.

## Chapter 10

### What happened in Ramallah in 1929?

**Status:** RESOLVED · printed

In 1929 our town had one Jewish household: a shoemaker the town called Abū Ibrāhīm, who worked in a shop belonging to the Orthodox priest Khūrī Yūsuf Ḥarb. That year a young man of the town beat him, and only the priest’s intervention saved his life. He moved his family to Jerusalem. In 1967 his grandsons came back to Ramallah to ask after the priest’s family and offer them help, as Ṭalʿat Ḥarb, the priest’s grandson, remembered. The month of the beating is not recorded.

**Sources:** Nayrūz (2004), pp. 303–304, from ʿĪsā Qandaḥ and the memoir of Ṭalʿat Ḥarb.

### How many of our men served and died in the First World War?

**Status:** RANGE · printed

The sources agree on “about thirty” but not on what the thirty were. Shāhīn and Shaheen count about thirty who died in the Turkish army; Nayrūz counts about thirty men of the town who went to the war at all. Every man of eighteen to forty-five was called; some paid the fine of fifty gold pounds and some hid in the vineyards. The family tree names five who died in Ottoman service, among them Khalīl Manṣūr and Buṭrus Ibrāhīm al-Saah, and about seven more who died in the war, place unstated. So the dead in arms were between five and twelve named men and about thirty remembered.

**Sources:** Shāhīn (1982), English, chronology; Shaheen (1992), p. 160; Nayrūz (2004), p. 56; Cadora (1954), p. 53; Ramallah Family Tree Book v117.

### When were our Christians first conscripted?

**Status:** RANGE · printed

The law came first and the call-up later. Christians were made liable by the law of 1909, though Shāhīn remembered it as 1905. By the Balkan wars of 1912 and 1913 Ramallah men such as Mūsā Ḥraiz were already in uniform. The great call-up came with the war in 1914, and for a while a family could still buy its son out a year at a time: Khalīl Manṣūr was bought out twice, and in the third year he was taken and did not come back.

**Sources:** Shāhīn (1982), Arabic, p. 60; Nayrūz (2004), p. 55; Shaheen (1992), pp. 160–61; Khoury (2022), p. 88.

### What was the radio raid of May 1944?

**Status:** RESOLVED · printed

On the night of 17 May 1944 armed men of the Irgun, the Zionist underground then fighting the British, broke into the transmitting station at Ramallah, disarmed the guard and tried to force the operator to put them on the air. When he refused they shot up the equipment and fled. On the road outside town the same night two Arab travellers were wounded. The British and the newspapers of the day disagree on how many attackers there were and whether any policemen were hurt.

**Sources:** Hansard, HC Deb 6 December 1944, vol. 406, cc. 557–61W; JTA, 21 May 1944. Web only; no Palestinian source found yet.

### Whom did the revolt of 1938 cost us?

**Status:** RANGE · printed

We can name one son of the town who died for the revolt: George al-Saah, “George al-Aqraʿ”, of the ʿAwwād, taken at Jifnā with a group of fighters and hanged by the British, and mourned for days in the Kharja square. Of those killed in the fighting of 1 October 1938 we have counts, not names: two women and three men in the vineyards, and from thirty to eighty fighters, as the witnesses tell it. After the searches the British left food stores ruined and some houses burned.

**Sources:** Nayrūz (2004), pp. 95–96, 113, 260–61; McDowell (1939), pp. 28, 45–47; Cadora (1954), p. 57.

### Who brought the election case of 1946?

**Status:** RESOLVED · printed

It was a petition against the election of Jalīl Ḥarb, the fourth winner, brought by men of the Jābir side. Saʿīd Buluṣ Jābir, a voter in the district, gave evidence as petitioner, the lawyer Mughannam argued for them, and the Atalla brothers argued to keep the result. It was heard before a judge between 29 October and 7 December 1946. The judgment has not been found, but the two families of Dār Yūsuf, the Jābirs and the Ḥarbs, held their feasts of reconciliation about 1949.

**Sources:** Israel State Archives, P-213/40, PDF pp. 42, 50, 53–55, 61, 69; Shaheen (1992), p. 220.

### Is the councillor of 1946 our historian?

**Status:** RESOLVED · printed

Yes. Yūsuf Jiryis Qaddūra was mayor from 1943 to 1947, as he tells it himself and as Nayrūz and Shaheen record. He stood in the Ḥaddāda district in April 1946, was elected second, served again as acting mayor in 1948, and in New York in 1954 published the town’s first history.

**Sources:** Cadora (1954), pp. 69–72; Nayrūz (2004), p. 387 and n. 65; Shaheen (2005), p. 17.

### Are the Ḥasāsna of the 1946 lists the Hasasneh?

**Status:** RESOLVED · printed

Yes. The column is headed الحساسنه, al-Ḥasāsna: the Hasasneh, the house of Ḥassān son of Rāshid. In court the chairman counted the same five men “from Haasassneh”.

**Sources:** Israel State Archives, P-213/40, PDF pp. 63 and 122; Cadora (1954), p. 31; Nayrūz (2004), p. 191.

### How was the 1946 election petition decided?

**Status:** STILL OPEN · not printed in the box

Unknown; no judgment is in the Atalla file. Either the petition failed or was withdrawn and Jalīl Ḥarb kept his seat, or his election was voided. The council of 1946 did sit (Khalīl Ṣalāḥ mayor in 1947), which leans slightly to the first (our inference). Exhibit P.5 (the register) and *Filasṭīn* of 7 April 1946 are not on the shelf. The file carries two case numbers, 197/46 and 199/46.

**Sources and searches:** ISA P-213/40, PDF pp. 10–11, 24, 45, 54; web: no reported judgment found.

## Chapter 11

### Who leased the land of al-Jalazūn?

**Status:** RANGE · printed

The camp was set up in 1949 on about 253 dunums of rocky hillside, first by the Red Cross, and UNRWA took it over in 1950. UNRWA’s lease was with the Jordanian government; most of the land was state land, and the rest belonged to families of Jifnā and nearby. One account gives the lease a term of ninety-nine years. No source produces the deed.

**Sources:** UNRWA camp profile; OCHA (2008); palquest; B’Tselem (2018, Israeli NGO, context only). Web only.

### When did Jordan’s radio leave Ramallah?

**Status:** RANGE · printed

Jordan took over the Mandate station at Ramallah on 15 May 1948. The kingdom’s radio opened its own broadcasting house in Amman on 1 March 1959, after a second studio there from about 1956, but the Ramallah transmitter kept working until 1967.

**Sources:** Cadora (1954), “The Broadcasting Station”; Nīrūz (2004), p. 344 and n. 30; Jordanian sources for 1959 (web).

### Did 1,500 of 6,000 leave by 1946 or by 1956?

**Status:** RESOLVED · printed

By 1946. Shāhīn’s table of emigrants opens with 1,500 in 1946, and his English text says the same.

**Sources:** Shāhīn (1982), Arabic, p. 22; English, p. 28.

### What did Ramallah record in November 1956 (Suez) and November 1966 (al-Samūʿ)?

**Status:** STILL OPEN · not printed in the box

Partly answered, still open for the town’s own papers: Barghouti remembers the town rejoicing at the canal’s nationalisation; the National Security Force drilled (Shaheen II); the Evangelical Home’s missionaries left (Nīrūz p. 369); Jordan made the district governor mayor in 1956–57; for 1966 only Anwar al-Khaṭīb’s account of al-Jalazūn. No Ramallah paper or council minute found.

**Sources and searches:** Barghouti, *I Saw Ramallah*, PDF p. 55; Shaheen II, PDF p. 100; Nīrūz pp. 369, 387; *JPS* “Jerusalem 1967” (2007). Searched: Nīrūz, Shāhīn, Farḥān, the Centennial Book, Kassab, Cadora, Shaheen I–II, Taraki. Wanted: *Filasṭīn*, *al-Difāʿ*, *al-Jihād* for those months.

## Chapter 12

### How many of the district died in the first intifada?

**Status:** RANGE · printed

The town has not yet gathered the names. In the uprising’s first two years, from December 1987 to November 1989, Israeli forces killed forty-nine people of Ramallah district. By the end of the uprising the district’s dead were probably between seventy and a hundred; that projection is ours. The popular committees were outlawed in the summer of 1988, on 1 July or 18 August by different accounts.

**Sources:** B’Tselem, *Annual Report 1989*, pp. 19–23 (Israeli NGO, used for its count); Johnson, *JPS* 18 (1989), p. 94; Tamari, *JPS* 20 (1991), n. 39.

### When was the Federation incorporated?

**Status:** RANGE · printed

In Michigan in 1959, the year the clubs met in Detroit and resolved to unite; Shāhīn has 1958. The government recognised it as a non-profit in August 1963.

**Sources:** Shaheen (2005), pp. 354–55; Shāhīn (1982), English, chronology; ProPublica, EIN 38-6096448 (web).

### When did Ramallah’s Muslims become the majority?

**Status:** RANGE · printed

In Jordan’s census of 1961 Ramallah was still 56 Christians in every hundred, and by 1997 it was 32. No count in between asked anyone’s religion, but after 1967 the Christian number could only fall while the town grew around it. Muslims most likely became the majority in the 1970s, and certainly before the mid-1980s.

**Sources:** Nīrūz (2004), pp. 179–83; Jordan, *Census 1961*, table 2.11; Taraki, *JPS* 37:4 (2008), n. 21.
