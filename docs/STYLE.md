# How the book is written

*The Roots of Ramallah* is a history for the people of Ramallah and their
children. It should read the way a good teacher talks: warm, clear and
exact. Every sentence should be one a curious fifteen-year-old or a
grandparent could follow without a dictionary. It must still be one a
historian would accept.

## Who is speaking

- The authors write as members of the family: **"we"**, **"our families"**,
  **"our town"**.
- Don't refer to the book as a speaker ("this book argues", "this book has
  been saying"). Say what we found: "We found…", "The record shows…".
- Don't address the reader as a juror, and don't write in the language of an
  audit ("tested", "verdict", "the claim fails"). That language belongs only
  in Part Two, and even there it should be plain.

## Sentences and paragraphs

- One idea per paragraph. Lead with the point, then the evidence.
- Prefer short sentences. Break a sentence that carries more than two
  clauses.
- Use plain words: "used" not "utilised", "about" not "approximately",
  "shows" not "demonstrates".
- No all-capital headlines. Entry titles are in sentence case. A short
  phrase, an em dash and a plain explanation is fine:
  "The name appears — written down in 1186".
- Use bold for the one or two facts in a paragraph the reader must take away.
  Use italics for titles of works and for a short closing thought, not for
  emphasis everywhere.

## Explaining as we go

- Explain every specialist word the first time it appears, in a few words:
  *defter* (an Ottoman tax register), *waqf* (a charitable endowment),
  *qāḍī* (a judge), *ḥamūla* (a clan), *phylarch* (a tribal ally of Rome).
- Give a date in the common era first. An Islamic year can follow in the
  note.
- When a chapter opens a new period, say in one or two sentences what is
  happening in the wider world before turning to our town.
- Name places so a reader can find them: "Bayt Jālā, just west of
  Bethlehem".

## Names

- In the running text, write the family and town names as the families
  write them today: Kaddoura, Totah, Saah, Hishmeh, Mogannam.
- Write a historical figure's name the first time with its scholarly form
  in brackets, then the plain form: "Rāshid (Rashed) al-Ḥaddādīn", then
  "Rāshid".
- Keep the diacritics already in the text for Arabic names of the period
  before 1900. Don't add new ones to modern family names.

## Saying how sure we are

Every entry keeps its evidence badge: Documents, Attested, Classical, Oral
tradition, Scripture or No evidence. In the text, say how sure we are in
plain words:
- "The record shows…" for documents.
- "The family remembers…" or "As told in 1953…" for oral tradition.
- "Scholars agree…" or "Scholars disagree…" for interpretation.
- "We don't know yet." Say it whenever it is true, and say what would answer
  the question.

Never smooth a doubt away. Never add a fact, number, date or name that is
not already in the sources cited.

## Notes, figures and pictures

- Every claim keeps its note. Notes may stay in their present scholarly
  form, because they are for checking, not for reading through.
- Every map, diagram, table and photograph stays. Their captions follow the
  same voice: say what the reader is looking at first, then why it matters.

## Part Two, "How we know"

Part Two holds the argument behind the story: the missing centuries, the
Ghassān tradition, Karak and the verdict. It may be more detailed. It is
still written in the same plain voice, for the same reader.
