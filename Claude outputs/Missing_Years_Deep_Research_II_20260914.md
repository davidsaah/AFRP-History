# The Missing Years — Deep Research II
**14 September 2026** · supersedes and substantially rewrites `claude/Missing_Years_Deep_Research.md` (19 August 2026)

---

## The headline

**The documentary void is not six hundred years. It is closer to two hundred.**

The August pass defined the problem as a silence of roughly 608 years between the last securely named Ghassanid and Rāshid (fl. c. 1500). That framing was wrong, and it was wrong in the family's favour — but only up to a point, and the point where it stops is now much sharper than it was.

Ghassān appears as a **living, located population** in al-Qalqashandī's *Qalāʾid al-jumān fī al-taʿrīf bi-qabāʾil ʿarab al-zamān* (c. 1400) — a book whose subject, stated in its own title, is *the tribes of the Arabs of the present time*. It places them at **al-Balqāʾ, the Yarmūk, and Ḥimṣ**.

That is not a literary memory. Al-Qalqashandī is drawing, through al-ʿUmarī's *Masālik al-abṣār*, on **al-Ḥamdānī the *mihmandār*** (d. 700/1300) — the Mamluk officer whose official function was to receive tribal delegations and therefore to know which tribes actually existed and where they camped. This is an administrative source, not an antiquarian one.

**So the chain now reads:**

| | |
|---|---|
| c. 945 | al-Hamdānī, *Ṣifat Jazīrat al-ʿArab* — *diyār Ghassān* named as territory |
| 538/1144 | Ibn ʿAsākir — a Damascene family nicknamed *Niṣf al-Ṭarīq* ("Half the Road") for an ancestor who set out after Jabala b. al-Ayham and turned back |
| d. 700/1300 | al-Ḥamdānī the *mihmandār* — Ghassān among the tribes of the present day |
| c. 1340 | al-ʿUmarī, *Masālik al-abṣār* — transmits al-Ḥamdānī |
| c. 1400 | al-Qalqashandī, *Qalāʾid al-jumān* — twice, at al-Balqāʾ / Yarmūk / Ḥimṣ |
| **c. 1500** | **Rāshid** |

The gap between the last administrative attestation of Ghassān as a people and the first man in our own chain is **about a century, transmitted through a source a further century older**. Call it two hundred years of silence, not six hundred.

---

## The counterweight — and it is a serious one

**Mujīr al-Dīn al-ʿUlaymī**, writing *al-Uns al-jalīl bi-taʾrīkh al-Quds wa-l-Khalīl* in **Jerusalem in 1495** — in **Rāshid's own generation, in Rāshid's own district** — mentions **Ghassān zero times**.

This was verified against a control term to confirm the search was actually reaching the text. It is not a null result from a broken query.

The man best placed in space and time to know of a Ghassanid population in the Jerusalem hills does not know of one. Whatever al-Qalqashandī's Ghassān were doing in 1400 at al-Balqāʾ and Ḥimṣ, they were not a visible presence in Mujīr al-Dīn's Jerusalem a century later.

**Both of these findings must be printed together.** The al-Qalqashandī attestation is the strongest single piece of evidence the tradition has ever had. The Mujīr al-Dīn silence is the strongest single piece against it. Printing one without the other would be exactly the kind of smoothing this book exists to refuse.

## Why the silence exists at all — al-ʿUmarī's own explanation

The most valuable thing in the Mamluk material is not the attestation. It is **al-ʿUmarī's category**.

He classifies certain Arab groups as ***ṣalība*** — lineages that had settled into villages and thereby "passed out of the status of Arabs" (*kharajū min ḥukm al-ʿarab*). They are still there. They are still known. But they have ceased to be the kind of thing a tribal register records, because a tribal register records tribes, and a village of farmers is not a tribe.

**This is the mechanism of the void.** The silence is not evidence of disappearance, migration, or extinction. It is evidence of a change in what the sources were built to see. A people who stop moving stop being written down by the people whose job is to write down people who move.

This single concept does more explanatory work than anything in the August pass. It should be given its own entry.

## Karak — the picture inverts

### The 1596 defter

**Karak in 1596: 103 Christian households against 78 Muslim.** A Christian *majority* in the town the family's tradition names as its point of departure.

⚠️ **Needs eyes-on verification at Hütteroth & Abdulfattah, p. 171** before printing. The figure came through a secondary route.

If it holds, it reverses the implicit picture the tradition carries — of a small Christian remnant under pressure. In 1596 Karak was a Christian-majority town. Whatever drove any family out of it, it was not demographic collapse.

Melkite Christians at Karak are independently documented in Arabic chronicles across the 13th–15th centuries. **Ibn al-Quff** — the physician and surgeon, born at Karak in 1233 — is described in the sources as *ملكي المذهب*, "of the Melkite rite." The Christian community the tradition presupposes is real and continuously attested.

### Musil, and the third account

**Alois Musil, *Arabia Petraea* III (1908), pp. 84–85** is the most important new source found in this pass, and it has not previously been in the book.

Musil did what no one else did: **he asked each Karak lineage where it came from, and wrote down what it said.**

The Karak **Ḥaddādīn** told him they came from ***b. el-Ḥaddād*** — a lineage name. **Not from Palestine.**

And Musil corroborates Burckhardt on the **Muslim** families of Karak (Hebron hills origin) but **does not corroborate him on the Christians.**

So there are now **three mutually incompatible accounts** of Ḥaddādīn origin:

1. **Ramallah's own tradition** — Ghassanid Christians, flight from Karak northward to the Jerusalem hills
2. **Burckhardt (1812)** — as read by the book to date
3. **Musil (1908)**, asking the Karak Ḥaddādīn themselves — descent from b. el-Ḥaddād, no Palestinian connection claimed

These do not reconcile. They should not be made to. Three accounts, printed as three, with the dates and the method of collection attached to each, is the honest presentation — and it is more interesting than any one of them.

## The name test, run properly

The August pass tested names impressionistically. This pass ran them as a controlled search against al-Maktaba al-Shāmila.

| Term | Hits | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| **الحدادين** | 553 | **Effectively all trade-plural** ("the blacksmiths"). Only three entries are family-name usages, and all three are 20th-century compilations. The word is an occupation, not a lineage marker, in the pre-modern corpus. |
| **الكسابرة / كسبر** | **ZERO** | Against live controls: **حلحول** 31 hits, **نوبا** 452. The search reaches Palestinian village names. It does not reach this one. |
| **رام الله** | **No pre-modern Arabic attestation at all** | |
| **دير غسانة** | **ZERO** | Confirmed twice, independently |

**What this means, stated carefully:** absence from Shāmila is not absence from history. Shāmila is a corpus of religious, legal, literary and chronicle texts — it is not a gazetteer and not a tax register. Small Christian villages in the Jerusalem hills are precisely the category of thing it would not contain. Dayr Ghassāna returning zero while being a perfectly real and substantial place is the proof of that: the absence is a property of the corpus, not of the village.

But the **الحدادين** result is different and does cut. It says that in the whole pre-modern Arabic corpus, the word functions as a trade name. Any argument that reads "Ḥaddādīn" in an early text as a family reference is reading a blacksmith.

## The Syriac silence is a positive finding

Six Syriac chronicles searched. **Ghassān: zero.**

But this is not a silence about Arab Christians. The same chronicles **name living Arab Christian peoples after the Islamic conquest, by name, in the same terrain**:

- **Tanūkhāyē** — 5,000 forced to apostatize at Aleppo in 778/9; the martyr **Layth** named individually
- **Tuʿāyē**
- **ʿĀqōlāyē**

At **Aleppo and Boṣrā** — exactly the country the Ghassanids held.

The Syriac tradition's vocabulary is ***Ṭayyāyē*** and ***Bēth Ḥārith***. It never says ***Bēth Ghassān***.

And critically: **Chabot supplies "Ghassanide" in a nineteenth-century footnote to a text that says *Ṭayyāyē*.** The identification is the editor's, not the chronicler's. This is the Western-editor-as-route problem in its purest form, and it belongs in the book as a worked example.

---

## The tradition has its own word for this

Arab genealogical science did not pretend chains were unbroken when they were not. It had a technical term.

***Nasab al-qaṭʿ*** — "severed genealogy." **Ibn ʿInaba (d. 1424)**: where the chain is cut, *whoever traces his descent to them must produce unusually strong proofs.*

And **Ibn Khaldūn, in the *Muqaddima*, names Ghassān** among the tribes whose lineages are confessedly mixed.

This reframes the book's whole treatment of the gap. The family is not applying a modern skeptical standard to a pre-modern claim. **It is applying the pre-modern tradition's own standard, in the tradition's own vocabulary.** *Nasab al-qaṭʿ* should become a named concept in the book — it is the most elegant available frame for everything the missing years are.

One further note: **Nikephoros I's Ghassanid-descent claim survives in Syriac** (Bar Hebraeus, p. 121; Michael the Syrian III.24) and is **early**. The genre of claiming Ghassanid descent is older than the void it now has to cross. That is neither support nor refutation — it is context for why such claims exist at all.

## Shahîd, and the limits of the modern authority

**Irfan Shahîd's "Ghassān post Ghassān" rests entirely on 11th–14th-century Arabic literature.** There is no contemporary documentation behind it. It is a reading of later texts, not a discovery of earlier ones.

And **Fergus Millar** (quoted by Hoyland) states the harder point: **no source from inside the Roman empire, in any of its four languages, says "Ghassanid."** The dynasty's own state never called it that.

This does not demolish the Ghassanids. It relocates them: they are an Arab-tradition category applied backward onto a Roman-period polity. Which is, again, exactly the situation the family is in — and saying so plainly is stronger than borrowing Shahîd's authority without its foundations.

## The generation-interval problem — and it is worse than we thought

The book's 66-generation count rests on a generation interval. That interval has no empirical basis for this population.

**No male-line generation interval exists for any Middle Eastern population.** ISOGG's own compendium of generation-interval studies is entirely European, circumpolar, and Chinese. There is no Levantine figure to use.

What Arab genetics papers actually do is **assume**:

- **Haber 2013** (the Levant, explicitly including Palestinians) — **25 years**
- **Fernandes 2015** — **25 years**
- **Zalloua 2024** — **30 years**

**All at or below the book's revised floor.** The assumption the book has been working with is not conservative; it is at the permissive end.

And a technical point that matters: **the Zhivotovsky clock is defined per 25 years, not per generation.** Any calculation that treats a Zhivotovsky-dated figure as a generation count has introduced an error of unknown size.

**Recommendation:** the book should state that the generation interval is an assumption, name the range actually used in the literature (25–30 years), show what 66 generations yields at each end, and decline to pick one. The honest output is a range, not a date.

## The Y-DNA claim will not survive citation

**J-Z640 has no peer-reviewed existence.**

- The sole paper is in a **Hilaris** journal — a publisher on **Beall's List** of predatory publishers
- Authors: **Michael Waas (University of Haifa)** and **Doron Yacobi (Tel Aviv)**
- Evidence base: **self-reported FTDNA customers** — no sampling frame, no verification, no controls
- **YFull dates J-Z640 to ~3,600 BP** — the Bronze Age, **predating any Arab tribal genealogy by two millennia**

A marker that coalesces two thousand years before the Ghassanids existed cannot be a Ghassanid marker. Whatever J-Z640 tracks, it is not this.

**Recommendation: do not cite J-Z640 in the book except as a worked example of a claim that fails on inspection.** Printing why it fails is more useful to the family than printing the claim would have been.

### And the deeper problem with the Palestinian genetics literature

**No Palestinian study distinguishes Christian from Muslim Palestinians.**

- **Nebel et al. 2000** (Hebrew University / Hadassah) — **Muslim-only by design**, and undocumented as to how subjects were recruited
- **Haber 2013** — resolves Lebanese Christians, Muslims and Druze as separate populations, but treats **Palestinians as an undifferentiated bloc**
- The **Arnaiz-Villena affair** — the paper deleted outright from *Human Immunology* in 2001 — is the canonical instance of how politically compromised this literature is

There is no genetic study that could, even in principle, speak to the question the family is asking, because no study has ever sampled Palestinian Christians as Palestinian Christians. That absence is itself a finding about the field, and it belongs in the book.
